How Your Choice of Shiplift Affects OPEX

Shiplift vs. floating drydock vs. graving dock vs. slipway — where the real operating costs hide, and why the most profitable yards keep re-engineering this decision.

Get a Yard-Specific Feasibility Study Why Pearlson

Every owner asks the same question at the start: "What does it cost?" The honest answer is that the capex is memorable but the opex is expensive. Over a 30-year yard life, the difference in annual operating cost between docking methods can exceed the original purchase price of the equipment — in either direction.

What is OPEX in a drydocking context?

OPEX is the recurring cost of running and maintaining your docking asset every year: electricity for pumps and drives, corrosion and underwater maintenance, docking crews and tugs, consumables, and — the biggest and most overlooked line — the cost of vessel time your customers spend in the yard instead of in service.

The four cost drivers, in plain terms

⚡

Energy & Pumping

Every docking in a graving or floating dock means moving thousands of tonnes of water — pump it out, pump it back in. That electricity bill repeats on every single docking. A shiplift's hoists are land-based electric machinery: no water is moved at all.

🛠️

Maintenance & Corrosion

Equipment that lives underwater corrodes all year long. Floating docks need regular drydocking themselves for painting and hull renewal on top of pump, valve and ballast-system upkeep. A shiplift's moving parts are mostly above the waterline and follow standard industrial maintenance schedules.

👷

Staffing & Docking Cycles

Moving-dock operations need pilots, tugs and marine crews; graving docks need lock and valve crews and long flood/drain cycles; slipways need dedicated hauling gangs. A shiplift is operated by a small dockmaster team, and a cycle — lift, transfer, berth — is measured in minutes, not hours.

⏱️

Throughput & Vessel Downtime

The hidden line. Every hour a commercial or naval vessel is out of service is money owed to the customer. A shiplift frees the platform the moment a vessel transfers to a work berth — so the lift starts on the next vessel while the first is still being repaired. That is why busy yards outgrow every other method.

The payback logic most yards miss

An empty docking asset is a standing cost. A dock that handles 3 vessels a quarter and a dock that handles 3 vessels a week pay the same bill for wharf, pumps and crews — but spread very differently across each haul-out. Because a shiplift transfers vessels onto land and re-frees the platform, it converts a chronic cost into a throughput machine. That is why the four largest shiplifts ever built — BAE, Austal, and the Adelaide record — were all specified by yards whose business depends on volume.

Honestly: when other methods still win

No method is universally best. A graving dock is unbeatable for large newbuild series and heavy offshore fabrication. A floating drydock remains practical for open-water seasonal yards that tow between sites. A slipway is fine for small shallow-water yards and fishing fleets. The question is not "which is the biggest machine" — it is "which asset turns your specific vessel mix into the most dockings, with the least recurring cost?"

That is a calculation worth doing on paper before you spend a single pound, dirham or dollar on steel.
